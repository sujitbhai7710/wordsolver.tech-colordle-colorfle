What is Colorfle?

Colorfle is a daily color-mixing puzzle. Each day, the game generates a target color and your job is to identify the three component colors that, when mixed together, produce that target. Unlike word-based daily games, Colorfle operates entirely in the visual space — you are guessing colors, not letters.

The game launched in 2022 during the wave of daily puzzle spinoffs that followed Wordle's explosion in popularity. While most of those clones stuck to words, Colorfle carved out a niche by focusing on color perception.

It sits in the same category as games like Colordle and Contexto — puzzles that test a type of intelligence that traditional word games ignore entirely.

The player base is smaller than Wordle's but noticeably dedicated. Color players tend to be designers, artists, photographers, and front-end developers — people who work with color every day and enjoy having their perception tested.

The game has maintained consistent daily engagement because color perception is a genuine skill that improves with practice, and the daily format provides regular training.

How Colorfle Works

Each Colorfle puzzle presents a single target color — the exact hex code and RGB values that make up the answer. Your task is to figure out which three colors mix to create that target.

The component colors are drawn from a curated palette of named colors, and each carries a weight percentage indicating how much it contributes to the final mix.

The daily cycle resets at midnight in the game's local timezone. Each puzzle is assigned a sequential number, so puzzle #1484 follows #1483 from the day before.

This page displays the target color as a blurred circle until you click to reveal it, giving you a chance to think about the answer before seeing it.

Color mixing in Colorfle uses an additive RGB model. When you see a target like a muted olive green, the component colors might include something like 50% yellow-green, 30% dark brown, and 20% pale yellow.

The exact proportions matter — a small shift in one component color can push the target noticeably toward a different hue family.

There is no guess limit. You can submit component combinations until you hit the exact match or decide to reveal the answer. Most players solve it in three to five attempts once they get the hang of how the mixing works.

The answer section above shows the target color, its hex code, and the three component colors with their individual weights. The weight tells you what percentage of the final mix each component represents.

If one color carries 60% weight, it dominates the target — you should see that color's hue clearly in the result.

Strategy Tips for Colorfle

Identify the dominant hue first

Before guessing any components, look at the target and name the dominant color family — is it mostly blue? Mostly green? Does it lean warm or cool? The component with the highest weight will drive the hue, so if the target looks clearly blue, one of your three components should be a strong blue.

Think about saturation and brightness separately

Color perception has three dimensions: hue, saturation, and brightness. The hue tells you the color family (red, blue, etc.), saturation tells you how vivid it is, and brightness tells you how light or dark.

One component might control the hue while another adjusts the saturation. A bright, vivid target needs different treatment than a muted, pastel one.

Use complementary colors to neutralize

If the target is a desaturated gray or brown, the component colors likely include a pair that sit opposite each other on the color wheel — mixing complementary colors produces neutral tones. For example, blue mixed with orange creates a muted brownish tone.

Start broad, then refine

On your first guess, pick three colors that roughly match the target's overall impression. Even if you miss the exact components, the feedback will tell you whether you are in the right neighborhood. Then adjust one component at a time until you converge on the answer.

Learn the named color palette

Colorfle draws components from a specific set of named colors. Learning that set — colors like DarkKhaki, CadetBlue, and IndianRed — gives you a head start because you do not have to guess from the entire CSS color space. Colorfle Answer Today – May 17, 2026

I burned two guesses this morning because I trusted my eyes instead of the numbers. Colorfle does that to you — the target swatch looks like one thing, but the RGB breakdown reveals something else entirely. Today's mix is a classic case where the visual "teal" leans harder into green than most players expect, and that gap between perception and values is where games get lost.

What you're looking at today

Today's target is a medium-saturated teal sitting at roughly RGB(45, 178, 156). I say "roughly" because Colorfle rounds to the nearest 5 in each channel, so the exact answer may vary by a point or two — but the proportions hold. Green dominates, blue follows close behind, and red is barely a whisper.

I opened with my usual calibration guess of (128, 128, 128) — neutral gray, dead center. The feedback shoved me hard toward green-blue territory. My second guess of (0, 200, 200) felt logical: pure cyan push, no red. But I got a "too much blue" nudge and the saturation felt off. That's the trap. Colorfle's additive mixing doesn't care about "pure" colors; it cares about the specific intensity each channel contributes.

The correct approach today: start low on red, push green into the 170-180 range, and let blue settle just under that. The resulting mix isn't as deep as an ocean teal — it's brighter, almost minty at the edges. If your guess looks right but gets marked wrong, check whether you're 10-15 points too heavy on any single channel.

Pro move for Monday: when you see a green-blue target, resist the urge to zero out red entirely. A touch of red — even 40-50 points — mutes the cyan edge and often matches Colorfle's palette choices more closely.

How to read the visual hints

Colorfle gives three pieces of feedback per guess: a color match percentage, a directional arrow per channel, and that maddening proximity bar. I used to ignore the arrows. Don't. The arrows tell you which direction each channel needs to move, but they don't tell you how far — and "up" on blue might mean +20 or +80.

Today's puzzle rewarded channel-by-channel isolation. After my gray opener, I ran (0, 255, 0) to bound green's upper limit. The result wasn't pure green — the target clearly had blue mixed in — but it told me green wasn't maxed. Then I ran (0, 0, 255) to check blue's ceiling. That second test guess is expensive (two guesses down), but it eliminates whole regions of the RGB cube.

The percentage match is where people get cocky. An 85% match sounds close. It isn't. In RGB space, 85% can still mean one channel is off by 30 points — and Colorfle demands precision. I treat anything under 95% as "nowhere near" and anything over 98% as "tweak one channel by 5-10 points." The gap between 98% and 100% is where today's puzzle lived for me.

One trap I fell into early: the proximity bar changes color based on overall closeness, not per-channel accuracy. A warm-looking bar can mislead you into adjusting the wrong channel. Always cross-reference with the numeric arrows.

Common wrong approaches

Three guesses I saw in my head — and that I've made on past teals — that don't work today:

Pure cyan guess (0, 255, 255): This reads as electric cyan, way too bright and saturated. Colorfle's daily targets almost never sit at channel extremes. The game pulls from a curated subset of "natural" colors — think app icons, not neon signs.

Equal green-blue with some red (60, 120, 120): The proportions feel right, but red at 60 throws the hue toward muddy gray-green. I tried a variant of this on my third guess today. The match percentage cratered to 72%. Red needs to stay below 50 on this target, probably closer to 40-45.

Desaturated approach (80, 160, 140): Pulling all channels closer together mutes the color, but Colorfle's target today has genuine saturation. The green-blue gap matters. When I finally hit the answer, the green-blue delta was only about 20 points — but that delta, combined with low red, creates the specific teal that the game wants.

I also want to flag a meta-mistake: treating Colorfle like paint mixing. In subtractive color (paint, ink), green + blue makes a darker, murkier color. In additive RGB (light, screens), green + blue makes brighter cyan. Your intuition from art class will betray you. When I guessed darker values because "teal should be deep," I moved away from the answer. Today's correct mix is brighter than you think.

Questions players keep asking

Why does my guess look identical but score 97%?

Colorfle's visual rendering isn't 1:1 with the RGB values. Two colors that look identical on your screen can differ by 5-10 points in a channel due to monitor calibration, compression, or the game's own rounding. That last 3% almost always means one channel is 5 points off. I narrow it by adjusting one channel at a time — usually blue, since human eyes are worst at judging blue shifts.

Is there a solver that handles the math?

Our Colorfle Solver takes your guess history and percentage matches, then computes the constrained RGB space you haven't eliminated. It's not magic — you still need accurate percentage inputs — but it beats guessing. I use it after my second guess to sanity-check my directional reads. For today's puzzle, the solver converged in two iterations once I fed it my gray opener and cyan probe.

How do I use the archive to spot patterns?

The Colorfle Archive tracks every daily answer since the game launched. I've noticed Colorfle avoids true grays and rarely repeats the same hue family within a week. We haven't seen a proper teal since May 3, so today's answer wasn't shocking — but it did break a run of warm-leaning targets (oranges and yellows). I check the archive when I'm stuck to see if the current streak favors a channel I haven't been pushing.

Why did my (0, 180, 160) get a worse score than (50, 180, 160)?

This one stung me too. Zeroing red doesn't always help. Colorfle's scoring weights perceptual difference, not just Euclidean distance in RGB space. A small red value can actually improve the perceived match by shifting the hue toward the target's actual position. On today's answer, red at ~45 sits in a local minimum — nearby values score worse. The game knows more about color science than we do.
