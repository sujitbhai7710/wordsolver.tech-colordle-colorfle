Reading the shape today

Worldle shows you one country silhouette and six guesses to name it. After each wrong answer you get the distance, a direction arrow, and a percentage. Those three numbers are enough to narrow 190+ countries down to one — if you use them right.

The silhouette for puzzle #1580 starts the same way every day: a single shape, no labels, no neighbor outlines. If you recognize the shape immediately, great. If not, the distance and direction from your first guess will point you toward the right continent within seconds.

How to narrow it down fast

Your first guess should be a large country in the region you think the shape belongs to — Brazil, Russia, China, DR Congo, Australia. Big countries give you better distance data because they cover more ground. A tiny island nation as your first guess tells you almost nothing if you're wrong.

After that first guess, the proximity percentage does the heavy lifting. Above 80% means you're within a country or two. Between 50% and 80% means right region, wrong spot. Below 50%? You're probably on the wrong continent. The direction arrow confirms it — if it points southwest and you guessed a European country, you're heading toward Africa or South America.

Pay attention to silhouette size too. Worldle shows every country at the same zoom level, so a tiny shape means a small nation or island. A shape that fills the frame is Russia, Canada, Brazil, or one of the other giants.

The shapes that trip people up

Small West African nations — Benin, Togo, Guinea, Sierra Leone — all look vaguely similar on a silhouette. Central Asian countries like Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan can be hard to tell apart. Caribbean islands are notoriously difficult because many share a rough teardrop shape.

If the silhouette looks unfamiliar after two guesses, it's probably a small country in a crowded region. Use the distance to gauge which part of the world, then the solver to filter by continent and population size. The solver won't reveal the answer outright — it narrows the list until you can make a confident pick.

Questions players keep asking

What time does Worldle reset?

Midnight UTC. That's 7 PM Eastern, 4 PM Pacific, and 5:30 AM IST. Same moment worldwide.

How many countries are in the rotation?

Over 190, including territories. At one per day, the full cycle takes more than six months. You'll see mostly fresh answers if you play daily.

What's a decent score?

1 or 2 guesses is a standout — the shape was probably obvious. 3 is solid. Most people average 3 to 4. If you're consistently at 4 or below, you're doing better than the average player.

Can the solver help without spoiling the answer?

Yes. The Worldle Solver filters countries by continent, population, and area. Enter your guesses and it narrows the list. You still pick the answer — it just removes the ones that don't fit.

Worldle Answer Today — May 18, 2026

Today's Worldle answer and how to solve it using silhouette recognition, directional hints, and continent filtering rather than pure geography trivia.

Worldle Answer Today — May 18, 2026

Today's silhouette is a long, narrow strip running north-south with a noticeable western bulge. If you're staring at that shape and thinking "Chile," you're close on concept but wrong on continent. The answer is Peru.

I burned two guesses on this one. First instinct was Chile because of that same stretched-out Pacific coastline look, but the northern taper is blunter and the Andes shadow sits further inland than Chile's sharper mountain spine. Second guess was Ecuador, which shares the northern border, but the silhouette's southern extension is too pronounced. That western bump is the giveaway—it's the Sechura Desert region pushing out past the Andes foothills.

Where in the world is today's answer?

Peru sits on South America's western coast, sandwiched between Ecuador and Colombia to the north, Brazil to the east, Bolivia to the southeast, Chile to the south, and the Pacific Ocean to the west. The silhouette captures that distinctive boot-or-sock shape: narrow coastal desert, the Andes running parallel, and the Amazon basin fanning out eastward.

What makes Peru tricky in Worldle is how much it resembles its neighbors in silhouette form. Unlike countries with obvious unique shapes—Italy's boot, Japan's archipelago, South Africa's triangular southern cape—Peru's outline competes with Chile and Ecuador for visual confusion. The key differentiator is proportion. Peru is wider relative to its length than Chile, and lacks Ecuador's more pronounced northern protrusion.

Continent knowledge solves this faster than country-specific trivia. Once you lock it as South America, you're choosing between five western-coast candidates. The directional hints then narrow it: Peru typically shows up with arrows pointing west (Pacific) and percentage distances that sit mid-range to North American guesses, far to European ones.

Reading the directional clues

Worldle's percentage and arrow feedback works like a heat map. After your first guess, the arrow points toward the target and the percentage tells you proximity. Here's how to use it without overthinking:

  • Arrow direction matters more than percentage early. If you guess Brazil and get a southwest arrow, you're looking at Pacific coast countries, not Caribbean or Atlantic ones.
  • Percentage drops off fast with distance. A 20% reading from Colombia means you're still in northern South America. Same percentage from Spain means the target is roughly one-fifth of Earth's span away—useful for eliminating entire hemispheres.
  • Cross-reference two guesses. Brazil → 15% with southwest arrow, then Argentina → 35% with northwest arrow. The intersection is the Andean region between them.

Today's puzzle played out exactly this way for me. Opened with France (habit, wrong hemisphere), got a 12% southwest arrow. Guessed Brazil next, arrow sharpened to west-southwest at 18%. That vector pointed directly at Peru's longitude. Third guess locked it.

Mistakes that waste guesses

I've seen players burn four or five guesses on obvious silhouettes because of these habits:

Guessing microstates or island nations first. If the silhouette has significant landmass, Malta or Singapore won't help your directional calibration. Start with a large anchor country in the likely hemisphere.

Ignoring the silhouette's orientation. Some players rotate mentally and guess a country facing the wrong direction. Peru's northern taper is clearly toward the upper-left (northwest) of the silhouette. If you flip it, you might guess something entirely different.

Chasing percentages too precisely. The percentage is proximity, not distance. A 50% reading doesn't mean "halfway there" in miles—it means you're in the right ballpark of the globe. Use arrows for refinement, percentages for hemisphere elimination.

Forgetting archives exist. If today's puzzle frustrates you, browse past Worldle answers to train silhouette recognition. Pattern matching beats raw knowledge after about fifty games.

Questions players keep asking

Why does Peru look so similar to Chile?

Both share the Pacific coastline and Andes spine, but Peru's northern third broadens where Chile stays narrow. The Sechura Desert creates that western bulge Chile lacks. In silhouette, Peru's aspect ratio is roughly 1:2.5 (width to length), while Chile pushes 1:5 or more.

Is Worldle getting harder or am I just seeing more obscure countries?

Archive data suggests the pool hasn't changed, but player fatigue after long streaks makes familiar shapes feel harder. Countries you haven't seen in 200 games play like new ones. Check the daily answer tracker if you suspect repetition patterns.

How do I improve silhouette recognition specifically?

Spend ten minutes with a blank political map and trace coastlines without labels. Focus on proportional relationships—how wide a country is compared to its neighbors, not exact borders. Worldle's silhouettes are simplified; understanding relative mass matters more than precise geography.

Does the game repeat countries?

Yes, but not on a strict rotation. Some countries appear multiple times yearly, others rarely. Peru last appeared in late 2025, so its return after roughly six months fits the observed pattern. The full Worldle archive tracks all previous appearances if you're building prediction models.

Tomorrow's silhouette will likely be a different continent entirely—Worldle rarely runs South America back-to-back. Brush up on African or Southeast Asian shapes before you sleep.
