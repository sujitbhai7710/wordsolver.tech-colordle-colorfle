Colordle Answer for May 17, 2026 – Day 1514

Today's Colordle answer at a glance

Cobalt, #030AA7. Day 1514, May 17, 2026. This one's a classic deep blue with enough saturation to punch through but not so dark it ever gets mistaken for black. When I filled it in on my grid, the green row lit up fast — it's distinctive enough that once you land in the right neighborhood, the exact hue separates itself from lookalikes.

The hex tells most of the story: red channel at 3, green at 10, blue cranked to 167. That near-zero red and green means any guess with warmth in it — your teals, your cornflowers, anything with a yellow undertone — will show heavy distance on the score. I burned two guesses on day one of my Colordle streak thinking "blue" meant "throw sky blue at it." It doesn't. Cobalt wants commitment to the blue channel.

What kind of color this is

Cobalt sits in that narrow band where blue stops being playful and starts getting serious. It's the blue of industrial enamel, of old glass medicine bottles, of the expensive crayon you'd use for oceans on maps. Not navy — that's darker and leans toward black. Not royal blue — that's brighter and carries more of a red undertone. Cobalt is its own thing: vivid, slightly violet-leaning, but still fundamentally blue in a way that doesn't apologize.

The hue family is straightforward blue, but the fun part is how close it runs to yesterday's Blue Purple (#5729CE). That color had real red in it — enough to push it into purple territory. Cobalt shed almost all that red. So if you're coming off yesterday's solve with purple-tinted glasses, recalibrate. The game wants you to drop the red channel almost to zero.

How to narrow your guesses toward it

Start with a guess that splits the blue space. I like going with something like #0000FF pure blue or even #191970 midnight blue early, just to confirm I'm in the right depth range. Cobalt's lightness value is middling — it's not washing out toward white like last Wednesday's White (#FFFFFF) or punching high like a pastel. If your first guess returns scores suggesting "darker and more saturated," that's your cue to stop guessing sky blues and start drilling down.

From there, test the red-green balance. A guess like #000080 navy will tell you if you're too dark. A guess like #4169E1 royal blue will tell you if you've got too much red or lightness in the mix. Cobalt's specific fingerprint is that extreme blue dominance with just enough lightness to keep it from entering navy territory. When I played today, my third guess was #0047AB — a touch too light, a touch too saturated, but close enough that the score pattern made the final jump obvious.

Where players usually go wrong

The most common trap is overthinking the purple edge. Blue Purple was May 16's answer, and it's still fresh in muscle memory. Cobalt has a whisper of the same violet quality, but it's not committing to it. If your score keeps telling you "close on blue, but something's off," check whether you're carrying red from the purple family. Drop it.

Another trap: guessing black-adjacent dark blues. #000000 or near-black navies will score terribly on lightness. Cobalt isn't trying to be dark; it's trying to be intense. There's a difference. I see people in the Discord throw #0B0B45 midnight blue at it and wonder why the score punishes them. The answer is always: look at the hex, trust the middle values, don't chase darkness for darkness's sake.

Finally, don't sleep on the Colordle solver tool if you're stuck. I build my streaks by knowing when to grind manually and when to let the logic do the work.

How the score logic actually helps

Colordle's scoring isn't just "hot or cold" — it gives you directional information on three channels. When I guessed #0000FF early, the score told me I was oversaturated and slightly too light. That meant the answer lived in a narrower band: still maxed or near-maxed on blue, but pulled back on saturation and nudged darker. Cobalt fits that description exactly.

The score also punishes red and green disproportionately here. Any guess with balanced RGB — your grays, your muted teals — will show distance across all three channels. That's useful negative information. It tells you: stop guessing colors that play nice across channels. This color is unbalanced by design. Lean into the blue.

What I appreciate about today's puzzle is how the logic rewards a two-step process: confirm the hue family, then finesse the depth and saturation. Some days the answer is a weird beige that could be anything. Cobalt is honest. It knows what it is and makes you meet it there.

Questions players keep asking

Is Cobalt closer to navy or to royal blue?

Royal blue, but it's not a great match for either. Royal blue (#4169E1) is lighter and carries more red in its mix. Navy (#000080) is darker and more muted. Cobalt is more saturated than navy and deeper than royal blue. If you're splitting the difference, aim below royal on lightness and above navy on saturation.

Why did my purple-adjacent guesses score okay yesterday but fail today?

Because yesterday's answer was purple. Blue Purple (#5729CE) has a red channel of 87 — massive compared to Cobalt's 3. Your eye might read both as "blue with something else," but the math diverges wildly. The game doesn't care what your eye thinks. It cares about raw channel distance. Reset your red expectations to near-zero.

How do I avoid confusing Cobalt with other deep blues in future puzzles?

Build a mental map of depth. Cobalt sits at a lightness where it never reads as black even in dim conditions. Navy can. Midnight blue can. Cobalt stays visibly, aggressively blue. When you see a deep blue answer in the future, ask: would this color look wrong on a small car? Cobalt would look great. Navy would look like a hearse. That's your divider.

Does the hex #030AA7 have any useful shortcuts for guessing?

Notice the pattern: almost no red, almost no green, blue in the 160-170 range. Any guess with blue below 150 will score as too dark or too desaturated. Any guess with blue above 200 will read as too light or too electric. Aim for the 160-170 pocket. I keep a running note of "landmark" hexes for common puzzle answers, and #030AA7 is now filed under "deep saturated blues, medium-light depth."

Game overview

What is Colordle?

Colordle picks a named color each day and you guess what it is. Type color names like "crimson" or "sage" and get back a percentage showing how perceptually close your guess is. The pool is roughly 150-200 named colors from the CSS spec — everything from "red" to "cerulean" to "gainsboro."

Some days the answer is obvious ("olive" in two guesses). Other days it's a subtle shade like "misty rose" that sits near multiple other colors. That variance is what keeps it interesting.

Rules and mechanics

How Colordle Works

Type a color name, get back a perceptual similarity score. The scoring uses CIELAB color distance — not hex proximity. That means "navy" can score 85% against "dark blue" even with very different hex values. Today's answer is Pink Red (#f5054f), puzzle #1472. Resets at midnight UTC. No guess limit, but most people solve in 3-6 guesses.

Improve your guesses

Colordle Strategy Tips

Start with a primary color to establish the hue family

Your first guess should be one of the basic primary or secondary colors: red, blue, yellow, green, orange, purple, or pink. The percentage score you get back immediately tells you which hue family the target belongs to.

If "red" scores 70%, you know the answer is in the red family — now you just need to figure out whether it is a darker shade like "maroon," a lighter one like "salmon," or a shifted variant like "crimson." This opening strategy works because it covers the widest possible hue distance on the first guess.

Use the lightness signal to narrow saturation

Once you know the hue family, the next most useful clue is whether the target is lighter or darker than your guess. If you guess "navy" and score 60%, the answer is probably lighter — think "cornflower blue" or "steel blue" rather than "midnight blue." Most color pools organize into clear lightness tiers: pastel, standard, dark, and very dark.

Identifying the tier eliminates a large portion of candidates in a single guess.

Learn the boundary colors between families

Colors like "teal" sit on the boundary between blue and green. "Magenta" sits between red and purple. "Gold" sits between yellow and orange. When your percentage scores hover around 75-85% regardless of which neighboring family you guess from, you are probably dealing with a boundary color.

Knowing these in-between shades gives you a significant advantage because they are among the hardest answers to pinpoint with binary "is it this family or that family" thinking.

Check the hex code when you are close

When your guess scores 90% or above, the color is very close to the target. At that point, looking at the hex code of your guess compared to a color reference chart can help you name the exact shade. For instance, if your guess of "coral" scores 95%, the target might be "tomato" or "salmon" — both are near-coral but have slightly different hex values.

The Colordle solver on WordSolverX lets you compare hex codes side by side, which makes this kind of precision naming much easier.

Keep a list of uncommon color names handy

Colordle loves to pick colors with less obvious names. Words like "khaki," "sienna," "taupe," "burgundy," "periwinkle," and "mauve" show up frequently because they fill specific niches in the color palette that common names like "brown" or "purple" do not cover.

Spend ten minutes reviewing a color name reference chart — it will pay off within a week of daily play. The searchable archive on this page also helps you learn which names appear most often in the answer rotation.

Do not overthink near-misses

If your guess scores 98%, the answer is almost certainly a named color variation that is visually indistinguishable at normal screen distances. Do not waste three more guesses testing tiny variations — pick the closest named shade you can think of and submit it. The game uses specific named colors from its palette, not continuous spectrum values, so at very high percentages the answer is usually one of two or three nearby named options.

Guessing your best option at 98% is more efficient than trying to split the difference between two nearly identical shades.
